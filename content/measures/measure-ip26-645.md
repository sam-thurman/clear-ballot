## What it does

In 2026 the Legislature passed a law (ESSB 6346) that puts a 9.9 percent tax on the part of an individual's yearly income that is above $1,000,000, starting with income earned in 2028.[^explanatory][^bill-6346] "Income" here is based on the adjusted gross income reported on a federal tax return, with some adjustments, and the $1,000,000 threshold is shared by a married couple or domestic partnership.[^explanatory] Initiative 645 would repeal that tax before it takes effect.[^explanatory] It would also add a rule that no state or local government in Washington may impose a tax on individual income, or a tax measured by individual income.[^explanatory] It defines "income" for this purpose as any gain or benefit measured in money that comes from a person's capital, labor, property, or other source, and "individual" as a natural person.[^explanatory] The same 2026 law that created the tax also created or expanded several tax breaks, such as removing sales tax on diapers, over-the-counter drugs, and hygiene products and expanding the Working Families Tax Credit; the initiative does not repeal those breaks.[^explanatory]

## What changes if it passes / if it fails

If it passes, the tax on income over $1,000,000 is repealed and never goes into effect, the state loses the money that tax would have raised, and future income taxes are barred by statute.[^explanatory][^fiscal] The initiative takes effect December 3, 2026, and applies going forward, not to past years.[^fiscal] The tax breaks that were passed alongside the tax (the sales tax exemptions, business tax changes, and Working Families Tax Credit expansion) stay in place either way.[^explanatory]

If it fails, the tax stays law and applies to income earned in calendar year 2028, with the first payments due in April 2029.[^explanatory][^fiscal] Five percent of the money collected goes to an account that can only be used for child care and early learning, and the other 95 percent goes to the state general fund, which pays for K-12 schools, colleges, health care, and other services.[^explanatory] The ballot includes a "public investment impact disclosure" written by the Attorney General, which says the measure would decrease funding for public K-12 education, higher education, and human services (primarily health care).[^piid][^ballot]

## Fiscal impact

A fiscal impact statement is a report the state Office of Financial Management (OFM) must write for every initiative, describing the money the state and local governments would gain or lose.[^fiscal] OFM estimates the initiative would reduce state revenue by $11,432,414,000 over five state fiscal years (2027 through 2031), almost all of it from the state general fund.[^fiscal] By year, the loss is about $3.1 billion in fiscal year 2029, $4.4 billion in fiscal year 2030, and $3.9 billion in fiscal year 2031, with no loss in 2027 or 2028 because the tax would not have been collected yet.[^fiscal] The child care and early learning account would not receive $377,000,000 that the tax would otherwise have sent it in fiscal years 2030 and 2031.[^fiscal] The initiative would not change local government revenue.[^fiscal]

On the spending side, OFM estimates state agencies would save a net of about $150 million over five years, almost all of it at the Department of Revenue, which would no longer need to administer the tax for roughly 30,000 taxpayers.[^fiscal] The Department of Social and Health Services would lose a small amount of federal money it expected for collecting child support from state tax refunds.[^fiscal] OFM's estimate assumes that about 89 percent of each year's tax payments would arrive in April and the rest in October, and that collections would be 90 percent of what is owed in the first year and 95 percent after that.[^fiscal] The estimate does not count effects that would depend on what a future Legislature decides to do.[^fiscal]

## Arguments for

The official argument for the measure was written by Chris Gildon, State Senator, Republican, Puyallup; Jesse Proudman; Patti Mann; Viet Nguyen; Semmelle Abraham; and Julie H. Olds.[^arguments] They argue that Washington voters have rejected an income tax ten times over 90 years and that the Legislature enacted one anyway, so the initiative "restores your right to decide."[^arguments] They argue the tax will not stay limited to income above $1 million, saying lawmakers rejected chances to permanently limit its reach and could expand it to everyone.[^arguments] They say the Legislature has a history of moving money out of dedicated accounts into the general fund, and that voting yes repeals the tax while preserving the Working Families Tax Credit, paid family leave, and small-business tax relief.[^arguments] In their rebuttal they say the opposing side's claims are false, that the measure "was never about schools or diapers," and that Washington has record revenues.[^arguments]

## Arguments against

The official argument against the measure was written by Janie White, President, Washington Education Association; Matt Hipp; Rosa Potts; April Sims, President, Washington State Labor Council; Thom Garrard; and Lindsay Johnson.[^arguments] They argue Washington has one of the most "upside-down" tax systems in the country, where working and middle-class families pay a higher share of their income in taxes than the wealthiest, and that the initiative would make that worse.[^arguments] They say the measure cuts funding for K-12 schools, health care, child care, and other services by $13 billion over four years, which they say means larger class sizes, higher costs, and the end of free school lunches for many children.[^arguments] They say the initiative is backed by hedge fund manager Brian Heywood and could mean higher taxes for thousands of small businesses and 460,000 Washington families.[^arguments] In their rebuttal they say the tax only applies to people making more than $1 million a year, that any attempt to expand it would be decided by voters, and that the measure "blows a $13 billion hole in the budget."[^arguments]

## Impacts you may not be thinking about

The tax breaks passed alongside the income tax stay in place if the initiative passes, so the state would lose the tax revenue without also undoing the sales tax exemptions and business tax reductions that were meant to be paid for by it.[^explanatory] Washington State Standard reported that the 2026 law also repeals sales tax on most retail services starting in 2029, and that those costs were to be covered by income tax collections.[^wss-debate] The Department of Revenue estimated in July 2026 that about 25,000 households would pay the tax, up from 21,000 assumed when the law passed, and that it would raise about $3.1 billion in its first fiscal year; Washington State Standard reported that this higher estimate came from an improved economic forecast rather than any change in the law.[^kuow-revenue] The fiscal impact statement notes that the Department of Revenue has already received about $10.2 million for fiscal year 2027 to set up the tax, and that it is unknown how much of that would go unspent if the tax is repealed.[^fiscal] Repealing the tax also removes a class C felony for evading it, which OFM says would produce savings for the Department of Corrections that cannot be estimated.[^fiscal] Washington State Standard reported that even if the measure fails, a legal challenge to the tax's constitutionality could still block the revenue.[^kuow-revenue]

## Who is funding each side

Figures below are contributions reported to the state Public Disclosure Commission (PDC) for the 2026 election cycle, as of October 17, 2026; they include cash and in-kind contributions and are listed as filed.[^pdc-data]

Against the measure, the committee "No on 645" has reported about $12.27 million in contributions.[^pdc-no645][^pdc-data] Its largest reported contributors are the Washington Education Association ($3,024,436), the Washington Federation of State Employees ($2,501,817), the SEIU 775 Ballot Fund ($1,500,000), the SEIU Initiative Fund ($1,000,000), and the National Education Association ($1,000,000).[^pdc-data] A second committee registered against the measure, "Millionaire's Tax Washington," has reported about $243,799, mostly from Invest in Washington Now, SEIU funds, and the Washington Education Association.[^pdc-data]

For the measure, "Vote Yes Repeal the Income Tax" has reported about $1.99 million; its largest reported contributors are Steve Gordon ($250,000, retired), Lawrence Hughes ($250,000, retired), Madrona Venture Group, LLC ($250,000), Larry Gordon ($200,000, retired), and Gordon Truck Centers ($100,000).[^pdc-voteyes][^pdc-data] "Let's Go Washington (Sponsored by Brian Heywood)," the committee that sponsored this initiative and the two other initiatives on the ballot, has reported about $3.95 million across all three measures; its largest reported contributors are Brian Heywood ($607,136, CEO of Taiyo Pacific Partners), Lawrence Hughes ($250,000), Steve Gordon ($250,000), Robert Crow ($210,000, investor), and Kelley DevCom LLC ($200,000).[^pdc-lgw][^pdc-data]
