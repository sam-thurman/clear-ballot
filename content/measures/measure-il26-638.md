## What it does

Today, Washington school districts may offer sports to students and may have separate teams for boys and girls, and the state Office of the Superintendent of Public Instruction (OSPI) writes rules against discrimination in school sports based on sex and gender identity.[^explanatory] A school board may hand the job of running competitions between schools to a nonprofit such as the Washington Interscholastic Activities Association (WIAA), which must have written participation rules and may not discriminate based on sex.[^explanatory] This initiative would require those participation rules to bar "biologically male students from competing with and against female students" in team or individual competitions that are designated for female students only.[^explanatory][^fulltext] The Attorney General's explanatory statement says this "would generally prevent transgender girls from participating on girls' sports teams," at public K-12 schools and at private schools that compete through the WIAA or a similar organization.[^explanatory] It would also require any student who wants to compete in a girls-only event to provide, as part of the routine sports physical, a form signed by their personal health care provider verifying the student's "biological sex" based only on reproductive anatomy, genetic makeup, or normally produced testosterone levels.[^explanatory][^fulltext]

## What changes if it passes / if it fails

If it passes, school districts and the WIAA must change their participation rules to match the measure, and students seeking to play in girls-only competitions must get the sex verification form from their health care provider.[^explanatory] The Attorney General's statement says students "would thus have to undergo some type of medical evaluation of their sex" to participate in girls-only interschool sports.[^explanatory] The measure amends two existing statutes, RCW 28A.600.200 and RCW 28A.640.020, and adds a new section to the law on school athletics.[^fulltext] It would take effect December 3, 2026, and apply going forward.[^fiscal]

If it fails, current law stays in place: school districts and the WIAA keep setting participation rules, and OSPI's anti-discrimination rules, which cover gender identity, remain.[^explanatory] KUOW reported that state policy since 2007 has allowed students to compete on teams that match their gender identity, and that the WIAA says there are between 5 and 10 known transgender students playing on school sports teams.[^kuow-voices]

## Fiscal impact

A fiscal impact statement is a report the state Office of Financial Management must write for every initiative, describing any money the state or local governments would gain or lose.[^fiscal] OFM found the initiative would not change state or local revenue.[^fiscal] OSPI would have a one-time cost of about $21,000 to update its guidelines to match the measure.[^fiscal] Local school districts would likely also have costs to revise their policies, procedures, and rules, but OFM says that amount cannot be estimated.[^fiscal]

## Arguments for

The official argument for the measure was written by Alesha Perkins; Dori Whitford, a high school track and cross country coach; Carol Brown, Olympic bronze medalist in rowing; Erica Li, MD, pediatrician; Ahnaleigh Wilson, student athlete; and Jess Grant.[^arguments] They argue that female athletes deserve fair opportunities and that Title IX, the 1972 federal law, expanded athletic access for women by recognizing the need for a female category, which they say Washington undermines by allowing "males to compete against girls."[^arguments] They say approving the measure would align Washington with policies of federal law, the Women's Tennis Association, World Athletics, and the International Olympic Committee.[^arguments] They say medical clearance is already required to play, and that sex would be verified during routine primary care using birth records, medical history, or a standard non-invasive exam.[^arguments] In their rebuttal they say the measure "doesn't require invasive or expensive exams," that standard pediatric care already verifies sex, and that it "doesn't ban anyone from sports" but requires participation consistent with one's sex.[^arguments]

## Arguments against

The official argument against the measure was written by Justin Gill, President, Washington State Nurses Association; Courtney Normand, Planned Parenthood Alliance Advocates; Karen Crowley, President, League of Women Voters of Washington; Grace Shih, MD, family physician; Katie Johnson, RN, retired school nurse; and Paul Charbonneau, high school girls' wrestling coach.[^arguments] They argue the measure would require girls as young as 8 to undergo "intrusive medical sex certification" as part of sports physicals, bars using birth certificates, and applies to girls only.[^arguments] They say doctors and nurses warn it would subject girls to medically unnecessary genital exams, and that many of Washington's 147,500 girls without a regular family doctor would be examined by strangers.[^arguments] They say coaches and athletes believe the measure adds barriers for all girls to address a statewide ban affecting 5 to 10 transgender girls, with no room for case-by-case decisions.[^arguments] In their rebuttal they call it "the most extreme in the nation" and say it is opposed by pediatricians, nurses, parents, and sexual assault prevention groups.[^arguments]

## Impacts you may not be thinking about

The two sides disagree about what the verification requirement would mean in practice: the Attorney General's explanatory statement says students would have to undergo "some type of medical evaluation of their sex," the argument for says verification would happen during routine care using records or a non-invasive exam, and the argument against says it would require genital exams or genetic or hormone tests.[^explanatory][^arguments] The measure's text lists only three things a provider may rely on to verify sex (reproductive anatomy, genetic makeup, or testosterone levels), and the argument against notes it does not list birth certificates.[^fulltext][^arguments] The verification form requirement applies to any student who elects to compete in a girls-only competition, not only to transgender students.[^fulltext][^explanatory] The measure would also reach private schools that compete through the WIAA or a similar nonprofit, according to the Attorney General.[^explanatory] KUOW reported that, according to Ballotpedia, 29 states have laws or regulations requiring sex verification for sex-specific sports teams, and that voters in Arizona, Colorado, and Nebraska will decide similar measures in November 2026.[^kuow-voices]

## Related

The initiative's act title, "defending equity in interscholastic sports," is the same as HB 1699, a 2025 House bill that would have let school districts bar "biologically male" students from girls' competitions; that bill stayed in the House Education Committee and never received a floor vote.[^bill-1699][^fulltext] Two 2025 Senate bills on the same subject, SB 5097 (girls' athletics eligibility) and SB 5012 (organization of interscholastic athletics), also stayed in committee without a vote.[^bill-5097][^bill-5012] Because none of these bills reached the floor, no legislator on your ballot has a recorded vote on them; the voting record shortlist on candidate pages lists them for completeness.[^bill-1699][^bill-5097][^bill-5012] The full text of the initiative and the official statements are published by the Secretary of State.[^fulltext][^explanatory][^fiscal][^arguments][^pamphlet]
