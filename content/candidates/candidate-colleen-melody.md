## Background

Colleen Melody began serving as the 100th justice of the Washington Supreme Court on January 1, 2026, after Governor Bob Ferguson appointed her.[^courts-bio] Before joining the court, she served for 11 years as chief of the Civil Rights Division in the Washington State Attorney General's Office.[^courts-bio] Earlier, she was a trial attorney in the Civil Rights Division of the U.S. Department of Justice in Washington, DC.[^courts-bio] She graduated from the University of Washington and the University of Washington School of Law.[^courts-bio] After law school she was a law clerk, a lawyer who assists a judge, for Judge Ronald M. Gould of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit.[^courts-bio] She was born and raised in Spokane and lives in rural Western Washington with her husband and two daughters.[^courts-bio] In the Voters' Pamphlet, Melody says she taught at the University of Washington and brought pro bono cases, meaning free legal work, for immigrants and veterans.[^sos-pamphlet] Her campaign website says she has volunteered through the statewide Access to Justice Board and the King County Bar Foundation.[^site-about]

## Positions

Washington's Code of Judicial Conduct bars judges and judicial candidates from making pledges, promises, or commitments on issues likely to come before the court.[^cjc] The bullets below summarize the themes Melody emphasizes in her pamphlet statement and on her website, not policy positions.[^sos-pamphlet][^site]

- **Independent courts:** In the Voters' Pamphlet, Melody says state courts "must safeguard our rights with independence and impartiality."[^sos-pamphlet]
- **State-law protections:** She says Washington's laws provide broad protections in privacy, the workplace, housing, healthcare, and education.[^sos-pamphlet] Her website says courts must safeguard Washington's "longstanding protection of rights in the workplace, in healthcare, and other individual civil and constitutional rights."[^site]
- **Victims of crime and discrimination:** She says state courts must be places where victims of crime and discrimination may seek justice "with safety and dignity."[^sos-pamphlet]
- **Access to courts:** Her website says courts "must be trusted and accessible to everyone."[^site] It also says she advocates for using technology to improve court access, particularly in Eastern Washington.[^site-about]
- **Role of judges:** Her website says judges are public servants who must "work hard, listen, and approach each case with impartiality and humility."[^site]
- **Civil rights work:** In the pamphlet, Melody describes her career as a civil rights lawyer and says she returned wages owed to workers and protected employees from pregnancy discrimination and sexual harassment.[^sos-pamphlet]

## Affiliations and endorsements

Supreme Court justice is a nonpartisan office, so no party preference appears on the ballot for this race.[^kc-sample-ballot] The Code of Judicial Conduct also bars judicial candidates from publicly identifying as a member of a political organization, except as needed to vote.[^cjc] In the pamphlet, Melody says she worked to improve access to services in her rural community, including resources for seniors, healthcare, and childcare.[^sos-pamphlet] Her website says she has volunteered with the Access to Justice Board and the King County Bar Foundation.[^site-about]

In the pamphlet, Melody self-reports endorsements from every sitting Supreme Court justice, retired Justices Yu and Madsen, Governors Ferguson, Inslee, and Gregoire, Attorney General Brown, and the Washington State Labor Council.[^sos-pamphlet] Her website's self-reported endorsement list includes the Washington State Labor Council, Washington Education Association, SEIU 775, UFCW 3000, and Washington Federation of State Employees.[^site-endorsements] It also lists King County Democrats, Planned Parenthood Alliance Advocates, Sierra Club Washington State, National Women's Political Caucus of Washington, and the Confederated Tribes of the Colville Reservation.[^site-endorsements] The site lists more organizations, tribal nations, judges, and elected officials.[^site-endorsements]

## Record

Melody has served on the Washington Supreme Court since January 1, 2026, when she joined the court by appointment from Governor Ferguson.[^courts-bio] Position 1 is on the ballot for a two-year unexpired term, meaning the remainder of the term of the justice she replaced.[^sos-pamphlet] Before her appointment, she led the Attorney General's Civil Rights Division for 11 years.[^courts-bio] The court's biography says the division, during her tenure, co-led a multi-state lawsuit over access to abortion medication.[^courts-bio] It also says the division challenged federal policies including the travel ban, the transgender military service ban, an effort to end birthright citizenship, and the attempt to repeal DACA.[^courts-bio] Before that she brought civil lawsuits over discrimination for the U.S. Department of Justice.[^courts-bio] The biography lists awards including the Attorney General's Office's Steward of Justice Award in 2018 and the Legal Foundation of Washington's Charles A. Goldmark Award in 2018.[^courts-bio]

## Funding

As of October 15, 2026, Public Disclosure Commission filings show Melody's campaign had raised $270,077.74 and spent $110,500.72.[^finance] About 4.1 percent of contribution dollars came from small donors, meaning gifts of $100 or less plus the lump sums the campaign reports as "small contributions."[^finance] The largest single line in the filings is $10,295.85 in small contributions.[^finance] The top named contributors, each giving $4,800, are Lynn Prunhuber (retired), David Stobaugh (lawyer, Stobaugh & Strong), Ekta Saini (CEO), and Justice for All PAC.[^finance]
