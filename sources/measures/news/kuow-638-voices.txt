                                        This November, Washingtonians will vote on an initiative intended to ban trans girls from playing on girls’ school sports teams.

We wanted to know what people are thinking about the measure, so we asked a diverse group of local residents in KUOW’s Community Voices Network to weigh in. (Read here to learn more about the project and how we chose people for this story.) Our group was almost equally divided— for, against, and somewhere in between.

By way of background, Initiative 638 is sponsored by the conservative group Let’s Go Washington. The law would require student athletes who want to play on girls’ sports teams to undergo genetic or hormone testing or an examination by a health care provider to determine a student’s biological sex. According to the initiative's backers, the requirement would ensure “equity, safety and competitive balance” in girls' sports.

This would be a change to state policy, which since 2007 has allowed students to compete on sports teams that align with their gender identity. The Washington Interscholastic Activities Association says the current policy is aimed at “maximizing participation in extracurricular athletics.” The WIAA also notes there are between 5-10 known transgender students playing on school sports teams.

A recent Pew Research survey found 73% of Americans are uncomfortable with trans kids playing on sports teams that do not match their birth sex. Many people are divided on the issue of potential physical advantages of trans girls, although research on that question is limited.

According to Ballotpedia, 29 states currently have laws or regulations requiring sex verification for gender-specific sports teams. Voters in three other states — Arizona, Colorado, and Nebraska — will weigh in on similar initiatives this November.

Here’s what 10 Washingtonians had to say about the initiative.

            KUOW Shivani Singh on the balcony of her Queen Anne apartment 
    
    


Shivani Singh, 31, Seattle, WA

“Individuals who are trans women or those who identify as women should certainly be allowed to play in women's sports.”

Shivani Singh is an anesthesiologist who moved to Washington state three years ago after completing her residency.

She questions the basic premise of the initiative — that trans girls would have a physical advantage when competing in girls’ sports.

“It takes some amount of time and research and energy to… zoom out a little bit and understand that is not potentially true,” she said.

Trans girls undergoing hormone therapy are not biologically the same as cisgender men, she said.

“I think it is likely that there are gonna be aspects of… taking those hormonal therapies that can also potentially put someone at a disadvantage,” she said.

Singh is also concerned that kids would have to undergo a health exam to prove they are girls — either through a genital exam, genetic test, or hormone test.

She calls that “inappropriate” and says the proposed exam could open the door to “potential abuse by the system, which seems troubling.”

KUOW Photo/Deborah Wang
            
         Small business owner Jill Beck poses for a portrait in the living room of her Seattle home. 
    
    


Jill Beck, 55, Seattle, WA

“I feel like there needs to be a place where women can compete and they have a chance to win.”

Jill Beck is an entrepreneur who has worked in tech and finance. Originally from New York City, she followed her future husband to Seattle 24 years ago.

She calls the initiative banning trans girls from girls’ sports a “really complicated issue.”

“I am sensitive to the fact that trans people are more vulnerable than most of their peers,” she said. But she thinks the initiative addresses an essential issue.

“There are biological differences [between men and women]," she said. "So let’s just talk about that and be honest about it.”

Beck recalls learning about Katherine Switzer, the first woman to run in the Boston Marathon in 1967. At the time, women were not welcome to compete in the race, and an official famously tried to pull her off the course.

“Women have tried so hard to compete in a separate division than the men for so long,” she said. She believes those separate divisions need to be protected.

Even if there are only small numbers of trans athletes competing today, there may come a time when that is not the case, Beck said.

“When is the inflection point when we have to do something about this?" she said.

Beck believes there must be a better solution than current Washington state law, which allows kids to join the team that aligns with their gender identity. She would favor the establishment of an “any-gender league.”

                /
            
        
            KUOW Firefighter Noah Skartvedt stands in front of his Ballard garage. The mural is by the artist Henry
    
    


Noah Skartvedt, 35, Seattle, WA

“I’m going to vote against it, but I won’t be surprised if it passes.”

Noah Skartvedt is a firefighter for the city of Olympia. He lives with his wife and young daughter in Ballard, just down the block from where he grew up.

He plans to vote no on the initiative. He said he dislikes that he even has to vote.

“It’s definitely an issue that the right… loves to make an issue,” he said.

He particularly opposes the parts of the initiative that require students to undergo a genital exam or genetic or hormone testing to prove their biological sex. He calls that “disgusting and creepy and weird.”

At the same time, he questions the state’s status quo, which allows kids to play on sports teams that aligns with their gender identity.

“Their solution is, ‘Oh, anybody should be able to play on any sport,’" he said. "I don’t wholeheartedly agree with that either.”

Skartvedt said he thinks people on the left don’t understand that many Americans are uncomfortable with trans girls playing on girls sports teams.

“I do worry that the left is on the wrong side on this issue as far as not adapting and addressing the real concerns that people have,” he said. “There’s a reason there’s two categories in sports, for the most part, right? Boys and girls, men and women. If we kind of blur the line of who qualifies for each one, why do we even have two categories?”

Even though he is voting against the initiative, he said he won’t be surprised if it passes.

“I think that would be a serious wake up call for the left to figure out how to talk about this and how to kind of pivot on this," Skartvedt said.

KUOW Photo/Deborah Wang
            
         Michael Albrecht poses for a portrait with his dog Eudora in his Seattle home
    
    


Michael Albrecht, 25, Seattle, WA

“That one’s easy for me. I think it’s an easy yes.”

Michael Albrecht is originally from Bothell, and now lives in Seattle with his husband and dog. He works as a travel agent and is also undergoing treatment for a rare type of cancer that he has been battling since the age of 14.

Albrecht supports the initiative to ban trans girls from playing on girls’ teams.

“It’s about preserving women’s rights and women’s access to sports,” he said. “Everyone 20 years ago would be 100% in agreement that…there are biological differences between men and women.”

But today people are afraid to admit that because of “certain political pressures," he said.

Albrecht said he believes that supporters of the initiative will be smeared with accusations that they are “transphobic or hateful.”

“It’s not about hating our neighbors who are trans," he said. "It’s about having people with the same biological realities play with one another and preserving women’s spaces.”

Albrecht said he also understands progressives’ claims that conservatives are making this a “culture war issue” which currently doesn’t affect that many girls.

“I think even if we have one girl affected in this state, we can have a law that protects that one girl," he said. "Just because it doesn’t affect many people doesn’t mean we shouldn’t have a law or an opinion on this matter.”

Oak Simmons 
            
        Oak Simmons on a hike in Ancient Lakes in Quincy, WA. 
    
    


Oak Simmons, 31, Bellingham, WA

“I just flat out think that trans people are normal human beings who should be able to play sports.”

Oak Simmons is an electrical apprentice in Bellingham. They identify as non-binary and trans.

They think the initiative to ban trans girls from girls’ sports addresses a problem that doesn’t really exist, as state athletics officials say there are only a handful of known trans athletes playing in high school sports.

“There's incredibly few trans people," they said, "and not really an issue with trans people playing sports.”

Simmons believes that at the school level, every child should have the opportunity to access sports.

“I also was a kid once and I didn't come out until I was in my 20s. It’s difficult to be young and it's great to have healthy things to plug into,” they said.

At the same time, Simmons said in terms of higher levels of competition, for example in college or at the Olympics, “maybe there's more to be debated.”

Simmons worries about the effect of barring those few trans girls from playing on their former teams.

“I think to be singled out to that degree, I think especially during adolescence, which is such a tough time socially for most people, could be pretty devastating," they said.

But they also worry that requiring genital examinations or hormone or genetic testing could make all girls feel uncomfortable.

“Scrutinizing their bodies to this degree does have a pretty big negative impact on cisgender girls as well, because there is a lot of diversity in cisgender women's bodies too," Simmons said.

Photo courtesy of Shereese Braun 
    
    


Shereese Braun, 57, Federal Way, WA

“This one is… just a muddy, muddy one for me, and it has been since people began talking about it.”

Shereese Braun works in social services and is the mother of two kids, including a daughter who played sports in school.

She said the initiative is a “very hard one for me,” as she is a strong supporter of LGBTQ rights.

But she has questions and concerns about trans girls playing on girls’ teams.

“I am still wanting to understand if there is a different biological advantage if someone is a trans female, which so far to my understanding there is," Braun said. “If my daughter had had her heart set on a gold medal or even medaling and wasn't able to stand on the podium because a trans athlete who had a biological advantage because they were assigned male at birth kept her from doing that, how would I feel about it?”

For her, the question of equity is also a complicated one. Some kids might get a leg up in sports if they are able to receive private coaching on weekends. That also doesn’t feel economically equitable.

She is leaning toward voting for the initiative, but she hasn’t read the text yet, and she said her opinion could be swayed.

“I wish there was a way that we could honor a trans athlete's right to participate in the sport of their choosing and also protect the rights of girls and women who have a league that was structured to… as much as possible, create a fair playing field," Braun said.

Tony Lucero, 60, Snohomish, WA

“It’s not something that I’ve completely made up my mind on yet.”

Tony Lucero originally hails from Southern California. He does a variety of jobs, from working as a security guard at large music venues to making donuts at a corn maze.

He’s conflicted about the initiative banning trans girls from girls sports, which he calls “complicated.”

On the one hand, at the grade-school level, he says “let the kids play.” But in high school and above, when the stakes get higher, he believes opportunities should not be taken away from female athletes.

“To take away a scholarship or a state title from some girl that worked hard, you know, it’s kinda difficult. Even though it’s like a low percentage, that person needs to be protected too,” he said.

He said he hasn’t heard all sides of the debate. He wants to talk with some of his trans co-workers to get a better perspective.

He also thinks both sides are making too much of their disagreements over trans issues. For example, he finds it ridiculous that politicians are fighting over access to bathrooms.

“It's just a bathroom. Just put up a different sign and you're fine,” he said. “I think some sides are missing the point and some sides are making too much of a point, you know?”

KUOW Photo/Deborah Wang
            
         Chris Porter sits with his dog Sable in the backyard of his West Seattle home
    
    


Chris Porter 60, Seattle, WA

“I remain offended by the whole notion of it. It’s a red herring… and a scare tactic.”

Chris Porter is an Air Force veteran and trained nurse who works at a nonprofit that provides health care for communities of color and other marginalized people.

He believes the issue of trans women outcompeting in women’s sports is a non-issue.

It “hasn’t happened. And I don’t think it’s going to happen," he said. "And the numbers are so tiny that you have to wonder, what are we doing?”

Porter said the initiative offends him mostly because of the testing requirement that girls have to be deemed biologically female through a genital exam, blood test, or genetic test.

“It reminds me of when, during the period of enslavement here, Black women were showcased out on stage and had their genitals examined so they were deemed to be appropriate to have the children needed to continue chattel slavery,” he said.

Porter noted at the end of slavery, white people stoked fears that Black people were out to take their jobs.

When asked why many Americans support banning trans girls from playing on girls sports teams, Porter said people may have strong feelings about the issue because of “what they don’t know and refuse to learn.”

KUOW Photo/Deborah Wang
            
         Navy veteran David Larson sits in the backyard of his home near Bremerton, WA. 
    
    


David Larson, 61, Brownsville, WA

“I don’t want anybody to be denied, right? But there is a point where fairness must happen.”

David Larson is a Navy veteran who works as a paraprofessional helping severely disabled kids. He calls himself “liberal minded but highly fiscally concerned.”

He believes there are clear biological differences between men and women, and that trans women hold physical advantages over cisgender women.

“Studies have shown that men are physically stronger than women," Larson said. "Not necessarily smarter, but stronger.”

With regard to trans kids, he thinks schools should do everything they can to accommodate them.

“I don’t want anybody to say, ‘No, you can’t do that,’” Larson said. But sports are the exception, he said.

“It’s a boys team, it's a girls team, or it's a co-ed team," he said. "We can’t change the whole plan just for a few.”

He knows this will be hard for some trans kids if they are excluded from the team they identify with.

“And that sucks. And I wish I could help you," he said.

Larson's solution: Find a co-ed league to compete in.

In terms of how much conviction he has about this issue, Larson says he thinks it’s a question of fairness. But it’s not high on his list of priorities.

“Am I going to lose sleep over it?" he said. "Probably not.”

Bethany Pierce
            
         Bethany Pierce in her home in Puyallup 
    
    


Bethany Pierce, 28, Puyallup, WA

“It feels more of a power grab than anything. This is a talking point very common in right-wing spaces because it's something that they can make an enemy out of.”

Bethany Pierce is an artist and writer. She studied history at the University of Washington in Tacoma. Her dream job is to be a museum curator.

She identifies as queer and she has a niece and nephew who are trans.

Pierce takes issue with the idea that men are better than women at sports and that men and women need to be separated.

She said that in events like women’s soccer, “you will see women run through the field, knees bleeding…they spit blood…play on an injury. Where a lot of male soccer players get kicked in the shin, they're falling to the ground crying for the ambulance.”

Everyone has different aptitudes, she said, and if a trans kid wins in a sport, “It’s because this person just ran faster, did it better, got the most points. I don't think it has anything to do with being trans.”

She also thinks making it mandatory for kids to undergo physical exams or testing to be eligible for a single-sex team is a bad idea.

“It's invasive to the student, it's invasive to the school because no school wants to deal with this level of questioning with their student body," Pierce said.

She’s pretty confident that the initiative will not pass.

“I think enough people know a trans person or enough queer people to figure it out,” she said.

But if it does, she thinks it will result in trans kids abstaining from sports altogether, “to save themselves the heartache.”
